The lighthouse keeper writes the weather in a small blue book. Fog, wind, rain, clear; each word receives its own line, and each line receives the hour. The work looks simple, but the keeper treats it as a promise to every ship that passes in the dark. On calm evenings he also records the color of the water and the sound of the rocks, because those details help him remember which nights felt safe.

One evening the lamp hesitates. The beam flickers once, then again, as if the light itself is unsure. The keeper cleans the glass, checks the wire, and waits with a spare bulb in his pocket. The sea breathes against the rocks in a slow, heavy rhythm. Gulls have already gone inland. He does not rush. Care, he has learned, is often a sequence of small checks performed without drama.

Then the light returns, steady and warm. He writes the truth beneath the weather: care is often invisible, but it keeps a path open. After that he walks the stairs twice more, not because the lamp failed again, but because trust is rebuilt by repetition. In the blue book he adds a short note about the repaired connection and the exact minute the beam became reliable.

Near midnight a fishing boat shows its own small light far out on the water. The keeper watches until the boat turns and continues along the coast. He thinks about the sailors who never see his face and never hear his footsteps on the stairs. They only see a mark in the dark that says: here is land, here is caution, here is a place that someone still tends.

In the morning he copies the night’s notes into a second book kept for the harbor office. The pages smell faintly of salt and ink. A young assistant asks why he writes more than the required weather codes. The keeper answers that codes tell a ship what the sky is doing, while the extra lines remind the keeper what his own hands must keep doing. Between those two kinds of writing, the light stays honest.
